Module P1 · Practitioner complement

Drafting Decoder

Read drafted clauses word-by-word like a senior associate.

Learning objectives

  • Read a drafted Sale and Purchase Agreement (SPA) clause and identify, phrase by phrase, what each defined term, qualifier and carve-out is doing to the risk allocation between buyer and seller.
  • Decode the most-negotiated definitions and operative provisions a senior M&A associate will mark up first: Loss / Losses, Material Adverse Change (MAC) / Material Adverse Effect (MAE), Permitted Leakage, the compliance-with-laws warranty, the knowledge qualifier, materiality scrape, pro-sandbagging and anti-sandbagging clauses, basket and cap, fundamental-rep cap, reverse termination fee, the conduct-of-business covenant, entire-agreement and non-reliance language, and the exclusive-remedy / fraud carve-out.
  • Distinguish drafting words that look interchangeable but allocate risk very differently — "commercially reasonable efforts" vs "reasonable best efforts" vs "best endeavours"; "fairly disclosed" vs "specifically disclosed"; "in all material respects" vs an unqualified bring-down; "to the seller's knowledge" with and without a constructive-knowledge gloss.
  • Hold a senior partner's drafting line in a meeting — translate phrases such as "let's tighten the basket", "this rep is too broad", "we need a knowledge qualifier", "limited guarantee cap is too low", "double scrape", "save-for-fraud", "Permitted Encumbrances list is too thin" into specific drafting moves on the page.
  • Identify the four or five places in any clause where a word change has cap-, basket- or survival-level consequences — and explain to a non-lawyer counterpart exactly what changing the word changes about the buyer's residual recourse.
Why this matters in practice. Modules 07–17 install the concepts. This module installs the reading practice. A senior M&A partner does not redraft an SPA by reading it once; she reads it word-by-word, asking on each line "what does this defined term do for me, what does this carve-out cost me, where is the qualifier missing?" The user's strategic goal is to be the non-lawyer who can sit beside a senior associate and follow that read-through without being caught — to spot, on the page, that "fairly disclosed against the Disclosure Letter" is not the same as "specifically disclosed", that an "in all material respects" bring-down is buyer-unfriendly compared to an unqualified bring-down, that a "specifically disclosed" disclosure standard reverses the burden vs. "fair disclosure". This module is the bridge from "I understand the concept" to "I can debate clause drafting".
Partner phrasebook — the drafting-room sentences you have to recognise

This is what a senior partner says while reading a draft on the screen. Each phrase is a specific drafting move, not just an opinion — it points at words that should change. The forward-link in italics is the module that develops the underlying mechanic.

  1. "Let's tighten the basket — make it a true deductible at one percent." Convert a tipping basket (buyer recovers from dollar one once a threshold is crossed) into a true deductible (the threshold is gone forever; only the excess is recoverable). Numerically: at 1% of equity value, on a €500m deal, buyer gives up the first €5m. Module 13.
  2. "This rep is too broad." Usually means the general representation (e.g. "the Target has always complied with all applicable laws") is unqualified. The seller's drafting fix is to add a knowledge qualifier, a materiality qualifier, or carve out specific Schedule items. Module 12.
  3. "We need a knowledge qualifier on this one." Insert "to the Seller's Knowledge" (or "to the best knowledge of the Seller") in front of the rep — limiting seller liability to facts known. The negotiation moves to whose knowledge counts (named individuals? deemed knowledge after due inquiry?). Module 12.
  4. "Let's add a fair-disclosure standard." Replace "specifically disclosed against this Warranty" with "fairly disclosed in the Disclosure Letter" — opening the universe of disclosures from a one-to-one match to the entire Disclosure Letter, where reasonable inference suffices. Buyer loses; seller wins. Module 12.
  5. "The limited guarantee cap is too low." The cap on a limited guarantee by a sponsor (a guarantee of the buyer's reverse termination fee, typically 4–8% of equity value) is below the deal's regulatory-failure exposure. Push for a higher cap, or a separate antitrust-failure pot. Modules 08, P3.
  6. "Double scrape, please." A double scrape strips both the materiality qualifier and the knowledge qualifier for purposes of indemnification (so the buyer recovers the full loss without those defences) while leaving them in for the breach test (so the threshold question of breach still uses them). Aggressively buyer-friendly. Module 12.
  7. "Save-for-fraud." Shorthand for the fraud carve-out to the exclusive-remedy clause: the SPA is the sole remedy except in case of fraud (variously: actual fraud / intentional fraud / wilful misrepresentation). The carve-out's exact scope is the most-litigated boilerplate phrase in M&A. Modules 12, 16.
  8. "The Permitted Encumbrances list is too thin." The Permitted Encumbrances schedule lists liens, charges and security interests the buyer is willing to accept on the target's assets. Too thin = the buyer absorbs unknown encumbrances; too fat = the seller smuggles real impairments past the buyer. Read every line. Module 12.
  9. "Hell-or-high-water on antitrust, with a four-percent reverse termination fee." Compound move: the buyer covenants to do whatever it takes (divestitures, behavioural remedies) to clear antitrust, and if it walks away post-signing on regulatory grounds, pays 4% of equity value to the seller. The two are typically negotiated together. Modules 08, P3.
  10. "Sandbagging-friendly?" Whether the buyer can claim on a warranty it knew was false at signing. Pro-sandbagging (Delaware default) lets it; anti-sandbagging (English default after Eurocopy dicta and modern UK practice) does not. Look for the express clause; if missing, the governing law's default applies. Module 12.

The diagnostic sentence. A senior partner saying "this rep is too broad — let's add a knowledge qualifier with a fair-disclosure standard, double scrape on materiality but only for indemnification, true deductible at one percent, save-for-fraud carve-out from the exclusive remedy, hell-or-high-water on antitrust" uses ten phrases above. Re-read the partner phrasebook until you can hear that sentence as a list of specific drafting moves on a page, not a string of jargon.

How to read a drafted clause word-by-word

Senior associates do not read SPA clauses in the way a non-lawyer reads a contract. They read in five passes. Each pass focuses on a specific axis of the drafting:

  1. Definitions pass. Every capitalised word is a defined term. Read the operative clause, then jump to the definition, then back. A clause is only as wide as its defined terms.
  2. Qualifiers pass. Mark every "to the Seller's Knowledge", "in all material respects", "reasonable", "commercially reasonable", "best endeavours". Each one transfers a slice of risk from one party to the other.
  3. Carve-outs pass. Read every "except", "other than", "excluding", "to the extent", "provided that", "it being understood". Carve-outs are where the real allocation lives.
  4. Cross-references pass. Every "subject to", "pursuant to Clause X", "as set forth in Schedule Y", "in accordance with Annex Z" hides the operational machinery elsewhere in the document.
  5. Remedies pass. Ask: if this clause is breached, what does the breaching party owe? Damages? Indemnity? Termination right? Specific performance? Inside or outside the cap and basket?

The eight clause readings below model these five passes. Each clause is a real drafted excerpt (some lifted verbatim from the source deck, some from market-standard formulations). Each decode walks four to six key phrases and asks the questions a senior associate asks on first read.

Clause 1 — Loss definition

Clause: "Loss" or "Losses" — definitions section
"Loss or Losses means any actual losses, damages, liabilities, fines, penalties, costs and expenses, including reasonable attorneys' fees and amounts paid in settlement, suffered or incurred by either the Buyer or any of the Target Companies which is a direct consequence of a breach of any of the representations and warranties indicated in Annex 8.1, it being understood that, to the maximum extent permitted under applicable law, in no case any special, indirect, incidental or consequential damages, loss of profits or revenue, loss of goodwill, loss of business or of business opportunity shall be considered as a Loss."

Why this clause is the keystone. The Loss definition sets the universe of what the indemnification regime can ever pay for. Cap, basket, de minimis, survival — all are calibrated against this definition. Get it wrong and the rest of the SPA's protective architecture is leaking from the foundations.

Phrase-by-phrase decode.

  • "any actual losses, damages, liabilities, fines, penalties, costs and expenses" — "actual" is doing work. It excludes contingent and potential losses (that have not crystallised) and forces the buyer to wait for the loss to materialise before claiming. A buyer drafter would push for "actual or contingent"; the seller drafter just won by leaving "contingent" out.
  • "including reasonable attorneys' fees and amounts paid in settlement" — explicit inclusion of legal fees (essential — without this, the indemnitee may bear its own defence costs even on a winning claim) and settlement amounts (a settlement is recoverable under the indemnity, not just a court-ordered judgment). The qualifier "reasonable" creates a downstream argument about which attorneys' fees actually qualify.
  • "suffered or incurred by either the Buyer or any of the Target Companies" — the indemnified persons. Important: this puts the Target Companies themselves on the indemnified side, which matters when (i) the loss is at the Target level (a fine on the Target), and (ii) for tax — paying the Target directly may avoid a "circular" payment that would otherwise pass through the buyer's basis. A buyer would also want to add: directors, officers, affiliates — extending the universe of indemnified persons.
  • "which is a direct consequence of a breach" — the direct-consequence test. This is a tight causation standard: only losses that flow directly from the breach are recoverable. Combined with the consequential-damages exclusion below, this is a heavily seller-friendly causation regime.
  • "in no case any special, indirect, incidental or consequential damages, loss of profits or revenue, loss of goodwill, loss of business or of business opportunity shall be considered as a Loss" — the consequential-damages exclusion. This is where most of the buyer's economic exposure dies. If the breach causes the target to lose a key customer, the lost-profits claim is excluded. If the breach is a misstated EBITDA causing the buyer to overpay (the multiplier point), the loss-of-bargain claim is excluded. The buyer drafter has three counter-moves: (i) carve out the EBITDA / multiplier loss expressly ("a Loss shall include any reduction in the value of the Target as a direct consequence of a breach, calculated by reference to the multiple applied"); (ii) negotiate a separate "diminution in value" indemnity outside this exclusion; (iii) accept the exclusion but push the cap higher.
  • "to the maximum extent permitted under applicable law" — a severability / savings clause. In some civil-law jurisdictions, statutory provisions guarantee a minimum recovery floor that contracts cannot exclude (e.g., Italian Civil Code article 1229 prevents excluding liability for fraud and gross negligence). This phrase preserves the rest of the exclusion if a court strikes part of it.

What gets argued. Four points: (1) "direct consequence" vs broader causation; (2) the consequential-damages exclusion (especially the multiplier point); (3) whether the Target Companies are on the indemnified side directly (tax-efficient) or only the Buyer is (which then passes losses up); (4) whether attorneys' fees include the buyer's internal legal costs or only external counsel.

Clause 2 — Material Adverse Change / Material Adverse Effect definition

Clause: "Material Adverse Change" / "Material Adverse Effect" — definitions section
"Material Adverse Change means any change, event or occurrence (without giving effect to this Agreement and the transactions contemplated by this Agreement) with respect to the Target or its assets, operations, affairs, prospects, condition (financial or otherwise) that has resulted or that could reasonably be expected to result in a Material Adverse Effect.

Material Adverse Effect means any effect (without giving effect to this Agreement and the transactions contemplated by this Agreement) that is, or could reasonably be expected to be, material and adverse to the results and/or financial condition of the Target or its assets, operations, affairs, prospects, future financial performance, condition (financial or otherwise), except to the extent that such material adverse effect results from or is caused by: (i) any change in worldwide, national or local economic, political or regulatory conditions, (ii) war, military actions, acts of terrorism, natural disasters and pandemics, (iii) changes generally affecting the industry in which the Target [operates], (iv) the announcement of this Agreement and the transactions contemplated by it, and (v) changes in Laws, except in the case of each of clauses (i), (ii), (iii) and (v) above, to the extent it does not have a materially disproportionate effect on the Target relative to other comparable entities operating in the industry in which the same operates."

Why this clause is heavily contested. The MAC / MAE clause sits at the intersection of (i) closing-condition risk (a MAC failure means the buyer can walk), (ii) bring-down standard (warranties are usually re-confirmed at closing subject to MAE), and (iii) interim-period drafting (the conduct-of-business covenant is often pegged to "no MAE"). One word change here cascades through the contract.

Phrase-by-phrase decode.

  • "any change, event or occurrence (without giving effect to this Agreement and the transactions contemplated by this Agreement)" — the "without giving effect to this Agreement" carve-out. The deal itself, and the announcement of the deal, may cause customer attrition, employee flight, supplier nervousness — a buyer cannot then claim the deal-induced effects as a MAC. Standard, not negotiable in modern practice.
  • "affairs, prospects, condition (financial or otherwise)" — the prospects word is doing serious work. Without "prospects", a MAC has to be measured against actual results — making it nearly impossible to invoke before they materialise. With "prospects", a downward forecast revision (a major customer indicating it will not renew, a regulator signalling enforcement) can constitute a MAC even before P&L impact. Buyer-friendly drafting always includes "prospects"; sellers fight to delete it. Akorn v. Fresenius (Del. Ch. 2018) is the rare modern case where MAC was invoked successfully — and "prospects" featured.
  • "could reasonably be expected to result in a Material Adverse Effect" — the prospective trigger (vs the retrospective "has resulted"). Buyer-friendly: it allows the buyer to walk on a foreseeable MAC, not just a crystallised one. Sellers push to drop "could reasonably be expected" so only realised MAEs count.
  • "except to the extent that such material adverse effect results from or is caused by: (i) any change in worldwide, national or local economic, political or regulatory conditions, (ii) war, military actions, acts of terrorism, natural disasters and pandemics, (iii) changes generally affecting the industry, (iv) the announcement of this Agreement, and (v) changes in Laws" — the MAC carve-outs. This is the most-negotiated paragraph in any SPA. Each carve-out moves a category of risk from buyer (who walks) to seller (who is held to the deal). Post-COVID, "pandemics" is a near-universal carve-out — the buyer wears pandemic risk. Post-2022, "war" includes Russia / Ukraine implications.
  • "except in the case of each of clauses (i), (ii), (iii) and (v) above, to the extent it does not have a materially disproportionate effect on the Target relative to other comparable entities operating in the industry" — the disproportionate-impact carve-back. The buyer claws back the right to invoke MAC even within a carved-out category, if the Target was hit disproportionately worse than peers. Akorn turned partly on disproportionate impact — the target's compliance failure was specific to it, not industry-wide. Note clause (iv) (announcement) is not in the carve-back — the buyer cannot claim deal-announcement effects ever. The carve-back is itself heavily negotiated: how to define "comparable entities", whether to require quantitative thresholds.
  • "changes in Laws" — a particularly tricky carve-out for regulated industries. Pharma, banking, defence — a regulatory regime change can wipe out the target's value. Buyer drafters often try to carve back regulatory-change risk for industry-specific impact only, leaving generic regulatory shifts on the buyer.

What gets argued. (1) "prospects" — in or out; (2) the pandemic carve-out (now standard but still phrased differently — sometimes "epidemics, pandemics, and public health crises"); (3) the disproportionate-impact carve-back's quantitative threshold (e.g. "twice the industry average"); (4) whether a "material" qualifier is itself defined (some SPAs add a percentage threshold of EBITDA decline to remove ambiguity, but most leave it qualitative).

Clause 3 — Permitted Leakage / No-Leakage Covenant

Clause: "No Leakage Covenant" — locked-box deal
"5.1 No Leakage Covenant. The Seller covenants and undertakes to the Buyer that no Leakage has occurred during the period commencing on (and including) the Reference Date and ending on (and including) the Effective Date. This covenant is given as a separate and independent obligation and shall not be limited by any knowledge of the Buyer.

5.2 Leakage Indemnity. Subject only to Clause 5.4, the Seller shall indemnify and hold harmless the Buyer against, and shall pay to the Buyer, on a full indemnity basis, the full amount of any Leakage, whether directly or indirectly suffered by the Buyer or received by any Seller's Related Party or any third party. For the avoidance of doubt: (a) the Seller's liability under this Clause 5 shall not be subject to any threshold, de minimis, basket or cap; and (b) no amount of Leakage shall be recovered more than once.

5.4 Claims Period. The Seller shall have no liability under this Clause 5 unless the Buyer has given written notice of a claim to the Seller on or before the date falling thirteen (13) months after the Effective Date. … 5.5 Purchase Price Reduction. The Parties acknowledge and agree that any payment made by the Seller to the Buyer pursuant to this Clause 5 shall constitute a reduction of the Purchase Price and shall be treated as such by the Parties for all accounting, financial and tax purposes."

Why this clause is the locked-box's beating heart. The locked-box mechanism fixes price at a pre-signing balance-sheet (the Reference Date / Locked-Box Date) and abolishes the post-closing true-up. The buyer's protection between Reference Date and closing comes entirely from this no-leakage covenant — without it, the seller could strip cash out of the target between the price-fixing date and closing.

Phrase-by-phrase decode.

  • "no Leakage has occurred during the period commencing on (and including) the Reference Date and ending on (and including) the Effective Date" — the leakage window. Leakage is a defined term covering: dividends, distributions, share repurchases, payments to seller-related parties outside the ordinary course, bonuses to management beyond agreed amounts, professional fees relating to the deal that the target absorbs, etc. Look at the Leakage definition itself — what is in vs. out. Permitted Leakage (separate definition) carves out specifically agreed payments (e.g. a pre-agreed dividend, ordinary-course employee bonuses).
  • "This covenant is given as a separate and independent obligation and shall not be limited by any knowledge of the Buyer" — an explicit anti-anti-sandbagging drafting move (in this seller-favourable clause, the seller is conceding that buyer's knowledge does not bar a claim — unusual seller drafting; in a sandbag-friendly jurisdiction this is the express override).
  • "on a full indemnity basis, the full amount of any Leakage, whether directly or indirectly suffered by the Buyer or received by any Seller's Related Party or any third party" — a full indemnity recovers the full amount with no causation issues, no foreseeability filter, no mitigation duty. "Indirectly" closes a loophole: the seller cannot route the leakage via a related party or third-party intermediary.
  • "the Seller's liability under this Clause 5 shall not be subject to any threshold, de minimis, basket or cap" — the leakage indemnity sits outside the ordinary R&W limitation regime. Every euro of leakage is recoverable from euro one, with no cap. This is the buyer's leverage in a locked-box deal — without it, the seller could leak below the basket threshold and pocket the difference.
  • "no amount of Leakage shall be recovered more than once" — the no-double-recovery rule. Standard. The same leakage cannot be claimed under both the leakage indemnity and (e.g.) a separate financial-statements warranty.
  • "unless the Buyer has given written notice of a claim to the Seller on or before the date falling thirteen (13) months after the Effective Date" — the leakage claims period. Standard practice: 12–18 months. Long enough for one full audit cycle plus a margin to discover hidden leakage. Tight enough to give the seller closure on its post-closing exposure.
  • "any payment made by the Seller to the Buyer pursuant to this Clause 5 shall constitute a reduction of the Purchase Price" — the purchase-price-reduction characterisation. Tax-driven: characterising the recovery as a price adjustment (rather than damages) avoids potential withholding tax on damages payments and aligns the parties' cost-basis accounting. Standard in cross-border deals.

What gets argued. (1) The Leakage definition itself — every line is fought; (2) Permitted Leakage list — what the seller can extract without breach (a pre-agreed dividend? deal bonuses?); (3) the claims period (12 vs 18 months); (4) whether the leakage indemnity is the buyer's sole remedy for cash extraction or whether parallel R&W claims survive.

Clause 4 — General representation: Compliance with Laws

Clause: General R&W — Compliance with Laws
"The Target and its directors, representatives and officials have always complied and comply with all applicable laws, as well as with all orders, authorizations, licenses and decrees issued by any competent authority with respect to the Target."

Why this clause is dangerous as drafted. This is a textbook general (high-level) R&W: an unqualified, indefinite-tense, all-encompassing statement. As written, it imports unlimited buyer recourse for any compliance failure ever — including ones the seller could not possibly have known about, ones that are immaterial, and ones already captured in specific schedules.

Phrase-by-phrase decode.

  • "The Target and its directors, representatives and officials" — the rep extends beyond the Target itself to its individual directors and officers. Important: a director's personal non-compliance (say, a personal data-protection violation by an officer) becomes a Target-level breach. Buyer-friendly. The seller's drafter would push for "the Target [only]" or limit it to "directors and officials acting in their corporate capacity".
  • "have always complied and comply" — temporal scope. "Always" is dangerous. It captures the entire historic life of the Target, including pre-acquisition by the current seller (if the seller bought it from someone else). The seller's drafter pushes for: "during the [3] years prior to the date hereof" or "during the period of the Seller's ownership". Look at every "always" / "ever" / "at any time" in the warranty schedule.
  • "all applicable laws" — universal. No materiality qualifier, no jurisdictional limit, no subject-matter limit. A Spanish data-protection notification rule the Target overlooked, an Italian local-government licensing technicality, a US state-level tax filing — all in. The seller drafts in a materiality scrape: "in all material respects" or carves out specific categories: "other than as set forth in Schedule 12.A".
  • "orders, authorizations, licenses and decrees" — broad regulatory net. Includes specific operating licences (think pharma, banking, gambling) where loss-of-licence is existential. The buyer wants this; the seller may try to enumerate categories or limit to "material" ones.
  • "any competent authority" — the regulator universe. Includes foreign regulators, sub-national authorities, professional self-regulators. A US-target seller may push to limit to US authorities; a multinational seller will accept the broad form but limit to "material" non-compliance.
  • The missing qualifiers — what should be there in seller-friendly drafting:
    • "To the Seller's Knowledge" — the knowledge qualifier. Limits seller liability to known non-compliance. The buyer counters with a knowledge scrape — strips the qualifier for indemnification purposes (so a knowledge-qualified rep is the pre-condition for breach, but once breached, the indemnity is full).
    • "In all material respects" — the materiality qualifier. The buyer counters with a materiality scrape — same logic, strips materiality for damages calculation.
    • "Other than as fairly disclosed in the Disclosure Letter" — the disclosure carve-out. The buyer fights the fair-disclosure standard hard, preferring "specifically disclosed" (one-to-one match between disclosure and warranty).

What gets argued. (1) Temporal scope ("always" vs a finite look-back); (2) materiality vs unqualified; (3) knowledge vs absolute; (4) double scrape on indemnification (strip both materiality and knowledge for damages purposes only); (5) jurisdictional / categorical scope of "law".

Clause 5 — Knowledge qualifier (with constructive knowledge)

Clause: Seller's Knowledge — drafting variants
"Variant A (seller-friendly). 'Seller's Knowledge' means the actual knowledge of [Mr. X], [Ms. Y] and [Mr. Z] as at the date hereof, without any duty of inquiry or investigation.

Variant B (buyer-friendly). 'Seller's Knowledge' means the actual knowledge of [Mr. X], [Ms. Y] and [Mr. Z], together with such knowledge as each of them would reasonably be expected to have after due and careful inquiry of the Target's senior management and review of the books and records of the Target Companies in the ordinary course.

Source-deck variant (typical Italian drafting). 'To the best knowledge of the Seller, there are no events or circumstances which may give rise to any investigation, inquiry, enforcement proceedings or claim vis-à-vis the Target.'"

Why this clause is the single most-leveraged definition in the SPA. Every "to the Seller's Knowledge" rep on the schedule is calibrated against this definition. Move from Variant A to Variant B and you have, in one stroke, doubled the seller's effective liability surface. As the source deck puts it: "It is important to specify which individuals' or entities' knowledge is relevant."

Phrase-by-phrase decode (across the variants).

  • "the actual knowledge of [Mr. X], [Ms. Y] and [Mr. Z]" — actual knowledge means subjective, conscious awareness. The seller-friendly form. Three named individuals — the knowledge group — is typical for an SME deal; for a large corporate, the knowledge group may be 5–15 people (CEO, CFO, GC, divisional heads). Drafting move: read the names. Are they the right people? Has the knowledge group been gamed (e.g., the chief compliance officer is excluded because he knew about the issue)?
  • "as at the date hereof" — knowledge frozen at signing. If a knowledge-qualified rep is brought down at closing, the knowledge group is re-tested at closing too — unless this phrase locks it to signing. Buyer wants knowledge tested at both dates; seller wants it locked at signing.
  • "without any duty of inquiry or investigation" — Variant A's hard cap. The named individuals are not required to ask anyone or look at anything. Whatever is in their heads is the universe. Seller-friendly to a fault.
  • "such knowledge as each of them would reasonably be expected to have after due and careful inquiry" — Variant B's constructive knowledge standard. Imposes a duty of due inquiry. The named individuals have to ask their direct reports and look at the books. Knowledge gaps caused by failure to inquire are imputed.
  • "after due and careful inquiry of the Target's senior management and review of the books and records" — defines the inquiry's scope: which people to ask, which documents to review. The buyer wants this broadly defined; the seller (if forced to accept constructive knowledge) wants it narrowly defined ("senior management" only — not middle management).
  • "To the best knowledge of the Seller" (source-deck variant) — this phrase is ambiguous and a drafting trap. "Best knowledge" can be read as actual knowledge (UK case law leans this way) or as imputed/constructive knowledge (some civil-law and US courts read it as importing a duty of inquiry). Modern practice is to not use "best knowledge" but instead define the term explicitly in the definitions section.
  • "events or circumstances which may give rise to" (source-deck variant) — the rep is forward-looking ("may give rise to" future investigations). Subjects the seller to liability for embryonic risks the named individuals are aware of. Seller-friendly drafting limits this to actual current proceedings.

What gets argued. (1) The composition of the knowledge group (named individuals); (2) actual vs constructive knowledge; (3) scope of any inquiry duty; (4) whether knowledge is tested at signing only or also at closing; (5) the buyer's parallel push for a knowledge scrape that strips knowledge qualifiers for indemnification purposes (so the rep is qualified for breach but unqualified for damages).

Clause 6 — Materiality scrape (and double scrape)

Clause: Materiality Scrape / Double Scrape — indemnification regime
"For purposes of determining (i) whether any of the Sellers' Representations and Warranties have been breached, and (ii) the amount of any Loss that is the subject of an indemnification claim under Clause 7, in each case any qualification as to materiality (including the words 'material', 'in all material respects', 'Material Adverse Effect' or any similar qualifier) and any qualification as to knowledge (including the words 'to the Seller's Knowledge', 'to the best knowledge of the Seller' or any similar qualifier) contained in such Representations and Warranties shall be disregarded; provided, however, that the foregoing shall not apply to (a) any Representation or Warranty that uses the term 'Material Adverse Effect' as a defined trigger of a closing condition, or (b) any specific carve-out expressly identified as not subject to scrape in Schedule 7.X."

Why this clause is aggressively buyer-friendly. The single most powerful drafting move in the buyer's playbook. A double scrape tells the indemnification regime: treat every rep as if it had no materiality qualifier and no knowledge qualifier when calculating losses. The seller may have negotiated qualifiers into the rep schedule — but the scrape strips them out for damages. This is the buyer eating its cake (qualifier stays for the breach test, sometimes) and having it (qualifier gone for damages always).

Phrase-by-phrase decode.

  • "For purposes of determining (i) whether any of the Sellers' Representations and Warranties have been breached, and (ii) the amount of any Loss" — the two-pronged test. Both the threshold question (is there a breach?) and the quantum question (how much does it cost?) ignore qualifiers. This is the strong form. The seller may push to limit the scrape to quantum only (so qualifiers still gate the breach test): "For purposes of determining (only) the amount of any Loss…".
  • "any qualification as to materiality (including the words 'material', 'in all material respects', 'Material Adverse Effect' or any similar qualifier)" — the materiality scrape. The list of trigger words is exhaustively specified to prevent argument over what counts as a materiality qualifier. Note "Material Adverse Effect" — without this, an MAE qualifier in a rep would survive the scrape.
  • "any qualification as to knowledge (including the words 'to the Seller's Knowledge', 'to the best knowledge of the Seller' or any similar qualifier)" — the knowledge scrape. Together with the materiality scrape, this is the double scrape. Combined, they produce a regime where the rep schedule looks balanced (with qualifiers) but the indemnification math behaves as if it weren't (qualifiers gone).
  • "shall be disregarded" — the operative verb. The qualifiers are deemed not present. A buyer recovers the full quantum of loss without having to prove materiality or seller knowledge.
  • "provided, however, that the foregoing shall not apply to (a) any Representation or Warranty that uses the term 'Material Adverse Effect' as a defined trigger of a closing condition" — the MAE-trigger carve-back. Critical: without this, the bring-down standard ("subject to MAE") could be scraped and convert to an unqualified bring-down — a drafting accident the seller cannot accept. So MAE is preserved where it functions as a closing-condition gate, scraped where it functions as a damages limiter.
  • "any specific carve-out expressly identified as not subject to scrape in Schedule 7.X" — the negotiated carve-out list. Some reps (e.g., the financial-statements rep where "in all material respects" tracks GAAP / IFRS audit standards) are specifically negotiated out of the scrape, because the qualifier is doing real work that can't be stripped without breaking the rep.

What gets argued. (1) Whether there is a scrape at all (in seller-friendly markets it is dropped); (2) whether it is a materiality scrape only, knowledge scrape only, or both (double scrape); (3) whether the scrape applies to breach + quantum or quantum only; (4) the carve-back list (financial-statements rep is the most common carve-back); (5) the MAE-as-closing-condition preservation (always, when the SPA has an MAE bring-down).

Clause 7 — Pro-sandbagging clause (vs anti-sandbagging)

Clause: Pro-sandbagging / Anti-sandbagging — the express override
"Variant A — Pro-sandbagging (buyer-friendly, Delaware default). 'The right of the Buyer to indemnification, payment, reimbursement, or other remedy based upon any Representation, Warranty, covenant, or obligation will not be affected by any investigation conducted with respect to, or any Knowledge acquired (or capable of being acquired) at any time, whether before or after the date of this Agreement, with respect to the accuracy or inaccuracy of, or compliance with, any such Representation, Warranty, covenant, or obligation. The waiver of any closing condition based on the accuracy of any Representation or Warranty, or on the performance of or compliance with any covenant or obligation, will not affect the right to indemnification, payment, reimbursement, or other remedy based on such Representations, Warranties, covenants, or obligations.'

Variant B — Anti-sandbagging (seller-friendly, English default). 'The Buyer shall not be entitled to bring any claim under this Agreement in respect of any matter of which the Buyer (or any of its officers, employees, advisers or other Buyer-Side Knowledge Group) had Actual Knowledge as at the date of this Agreement, where such Actual Knowledge would have entitled the Buyer to bring such a claim if the relevant Representation and Warranty were qualified by the words 'so far as the Buyer is aware'.'"

Why this clause turns the deal upside down. Sandbagging is the practice of a buyer claiming on a warranty it knew was false at signing. The clause picks a side. The default rule depends on governing law: Delaware is broadly pro-sandbagging by default (a buyer can claim despite pre-signing knowledge — Cobalt Operating (Strine V.C., 2007); confirmed by recent Delaware cases including the Arwood line); English law leans anti-sandbagging by default (older dicta in Eurocopy v Teesdale [1992] BCLC 1067 and the modern UK practitioner consensus). If the SPA is silent, the governing law's default applies — but most modern SPAs choose explicitly.

Phrase-by-phrase decode (Variant A — pro-sandbagging).

  • "The right of the Buyer to indemnification … will not be affected by any investigation conducted" — the buyer's diligence does not bar claims. The seller cannot say "you investigated this and signed anyway, so you accepted the risk".
  • "or any Knowledge acquired (or capable of being acquired)" — note "capable of being acquired" — even constructive buyer knowledge (what the buyer should have found) is irrelevant. The strongest pro-sandbagging form.
  • "at any time, whether before or after the date of this Agreement" — temporal: pre-signing knowledge doesn't bar claims, and (more controversially) post-signing knowledge before closing also doesn't bar. The seller may push to allow post-signing pre-closing knowledge to bar a closing-condition waiver decision.
  • "The waiver of any closing condition based on the accuracy of any Representation … will not affect the right to indemnification" — the closing-waiver savings clause. If the buyer learns of a breach pre-closing and waives the bring-down condition (decides to close anyway), the buyer still retains its post-closing indemnity claim. Without this, the seller could argue waiver of CP = waiver of all damages.

Phrase-by-phrase decode (Variant B — anti-sandbagging).

  • "shall not be entitled to bring any claim … in respect of any matter of which the Buyer … had Actual Knowledge" — the buyer cannot claim where it knew. Note "Actual Knowledge" — usually defined to a Buyer-Side Knowledge Group (the buyer's deal team named individuals). Constructive knowledge of the buyer is typically not enough to bar a claim — that would be too punitive.
  • "where such Actual Knowledge would have entitled the Buyer to bring such a claim if the relevant Representation and Warranty were qualified by the words 'so far as the Buyer is aware'" — the bar tracks the disclosure standard. The seller is essentially saying: knowledge that would constitute fair disclosure if the seller had disclosed it bars the claim. A subtle but important formulation — it imports the disclosure-standard logic into the sandbagging analysis.

What gets argued. (1) Pro vs anti choice (the binary); (2) if anti, the buyer-side knowledge group composition; (3) whether the bar is "actual" or also "constructive" buyer knowledge; (4) the closing-waiver savings clause (almost always preserved even in anti-sandbagging form); (5) interaction with the disclosure regime (fairly disclosed matter typically can't be sandbagged in any case).

Clause 8 — Basket / Cap / Fundamental-Rep Cap

Clause: Monetary Thresholds — De Minimis, Basket, Cap, Fundamental Carve-out
"(b) Monetary Thresholds. The Parties agree that:
(i) Sellers shall not be liable under this Clause 7:
   (1) (De Minimis) If the amount due in connection with any Indemnification Event does not exceed € 15,000.00 (fifteen thousand/00);
   (2) (Basket) Until the aggregate of all amounts that would otherwise be due in respect of all other claims against the Sellers exceeds € 100,000.00 (one hundred thousand), it being understood that, if such limit is exceeded, the Sellers shall be liable for the whole amount of the claim and not merely the excess;
   (3) (Cap) For any amount in excess of € 1,300,000.00 (one million three hundred thousand/00) (the 'Cap');
(ii) the monetary thresholds provided in this Clause 7.4(b) shall not apply in case of any Losses arising out of (1) fraud or intentional breach or misrepresentation or (2) breach of any of the Fundamental Representations."

Why this clause sets the residual-recourse architecture. The triplet — de minimis, basket, cap — is the entire ladder of monetary protection and limitation. On a deal of, say, €15m enterprise value (this looks like a small Italian deal), the basket of €100k = 0.67% and the cap of €1.3m = 8.7%. Compare to market practice (broader European mid-market): basket 0.5–1% of enterprise value, cap 10–25% for general warranties (lower if W&I-insured, sometimes as low as €1 token cap with W&I taking the load).

Phrase-by-phrase decode.

  • "(De Minimis) If the amount due in connection with any Indemnification Event does not exceed € 15,000.00" — the de minimis: per-claim floor. Claims under €15k cannot be brought at all. Drafting questions: (i) does a single Indemnification Event encompass multiple related claims (an "aggregation" question — buyer wants to aggregate; seller wants each invoice-level claim to count separately)? (ii) Does the sub-de-minimis claim count toward the basket (sweep) or fall away entirely (no sweep)?
  • "(Basket) Until the aggregate of all amounts that would otherwise be due … exceeds € 100,000" — the basket: aggregate threshold. Sub-basket losses are not recoverable.
  • "it being understood that, if such limit is exceeded, the Sellers shall be liable for the whole amount of the claim and not merely the excess" — this single sentence makes it a tipping basket, not a deductible. Once the €100k threshold is crossed, the seller pays from euro one. Buyer-friendly. The seller's preferred drafting is the deductible: "the Sellers shall be liable only for the amount in excess of such basket" — meaning the first €100k is gone forever and only the excess is recoverable. The senior partner phrase: "let's tighten the basket — make it a true deductible at one percent" — converts buyer-friendly tipping into seller-friendly deductible.
  • "(Cap) For any amount in excess of € 1,300,000.00" — the cap: aggregate ceiling on seller liability. Above the cap, the buyer absorbs the loss.
  • "the monetary thresholds … shall not apply in case of any Losses arising out of (1) fraud or intentional breach or misrepresentation" — the fraud carve-out. Fraud breaks every limit. Note three flavours: "fraud" (narrowest), "intentional breach" (broader — captures wilful breaches that fall short of fraud), "misrepresentation" (potentially broadest — captures any false statement). The seller's drafter pushes for "fraud" only; the buyer's pushes for the full triplet.
  • "or (2) breach of any of the Fundamental Representations" — the fundamental-rep carve-out. Fundamental Reps are typically: (i) authority and capacity, (ii) ownership of shares free of encumbrances, (iii) no conflicts. Breach of a fundamental rep means the deal didn't legally happen — so no monetary cap protects the seller. Note the typical further negotiation: fundamentals are often capped at 100% of purchase price rather than uncapped (a seller would refuse to be liable above what it received).

What gets argued. (1) De minimis size; (2) basket size and tipping vs deductible; (3) sweep — do sub-de-minimis claims count toward basket? (4) cap as % of equity value (10–25% is mid-market for general warranties, lower with W&I); (5) whether tax warranties have their own cap (often 100% of price); (6) fundamental-rep cap (uncapped vs 100% of price); (7) the precise scope of the fraud carve-out (intent, "actual" vs "constructive" fraud).

Clause 9 — Conduct-of-business covenant (interim period)

Clause: Conduct of Business — interim period covenant
"From the date hereof until the Closing Date, except as (i) expressly contemplated by this Agreement, (ii) set forth in Schedule [X], (iii) required by applicable Law, or (iv) consented to in writing by the Buyer (such consent not to be unreasonably withheld, conditioned or delayed), the Seller shall procure that each Target Company:
(a) operates its business in the ordinary course consistent with past practice and uses commercially reasonable efforts to preserve substantially intact its business organization, keep available the services of its officers and key employees, and preserve its present relationships with material customers, suppliers, distributors and other business partners; and
(b) shall not, without the Buyer's prior written consent (such consent not to be unreasonably withheld, conditioned or delayed):
(i) declare, set aside or pay any dividend or other distribution …
(ii) issue, sell, pledge, dispose of or encumber any shares …
(iii) acquire any business or any corporation … having a value in excess of [€X];
(iv) sell, lease, transfer or dispose of any of its material assets …
(v) incur any indebtedness for borrowed money in excess of [€X];
(vi) enter into, materially modify or terminate any Material Contract …
(vii) increase the compensation of any director, officer or employee earning more than [€X] …
(viii) make any change in accounting principles or practices …;"

Why this clause is the bridge across the interim period. Between signing and closing, the seller still owns and runs the target. The buyer is contractually committed to buy, but operationally exposed: a careless or self-interested seller could damage the business, drain it, or fundamentally alter it. The conduct-of-business covenant is the buyer's tether — it locks the seller into running the business as before, with the buyer holding a veto on extraordinary actions.

Phrase-by-phrase decode.

  • "except as (i) expressly contemplated by this Agreement, (ii) set forth in Schedule [X], (iii) required by applicable Law, or (iv) consented to in writing by the Buyer" — the four carve-outs. Read each closely: (i) actions the SPA itself authorises (e.g., the agreed pre-closing reorganisation step plan); (ii) the schedule of agreed exceptions (the seller's negotiated list of "I plan to do these things between now and closing"); (iii) regulatory requirements (irreducible); (iv) buyer's consent. The schedule is where the real fight happens — anything the seller wants to do without further buyer consent must be on it.
  • "operates its business in the ordinary course consistent with past practice" — the ordinary-course covenant. The single most-litigated phrase in interim-period drafting. The 2020 Delaware cases (AB Stable v MAPS Hotels) construed "ordinary course consistent with past practice" tightly: the target's COVID-era pivot (closing hotels, reducing service) was not ordinary course even though the pandemic was extraordinary. The clause is read against pre-pandemic patterns. Drafting variants: "ordinary course" alone (looser); "ordinary course consistent with past practice in all material respects" (medium); "ordinary course consistent with past practice during the [12] months prior" (tightest).
  • "uses commercially reasonable efforts to preserve substantially intact its business organization" — the commercially reasonable efforts standard (lowest of the three rungs in the efforts ladder; see the cheat-sheet below). The seller is not required to suffer commercial harm to preserve relationships — only to take steps a reasonable similarly-situated business person would take.
  • "(such consent not to be unreasonably withheld, conditioned or delayed)" — the reasonable-consent qualifier. The buyer can deny consent only with reasonable grounds. Without this qualifier, the buyer holds an arbitrary veto and could weaponise it to renegotiate price or walk. Standard. Note "withheld, conditioned or delayed" — also bars the buyer from running the clock on a request.
  • "having a value in excess of [€X]" — the materiality threshold. Below the threshold, the seller can act without consent; above, consent is required. The threshold sizing is the negotiation. Too low: the buyer is consulted on every routine purchase; too high: the seller can do significant deals without consent. Typical for €500m EBITDA target: €1–5m for asset transactions, €0.5–2m for compensation, €5–10m for borrowing.
  • "any Material Contract" — definition cross-reference. Material Contracts are typically defined as contracts above a value threshold or otherwise key to the business (top-N customers, top-N suppliers, exclusive licences, non-competes). Look at the Material Contracts definition; the conduct covenant's bite scales with it.
  • "increase the compensation of any director, officer or employee earning more than [€X]" — narrowing the catch. Below €X, no consent needed. Standard practice keeps routine raises out of the consent regime.

What gets argued. (1) The schedule of agreed exceptions (every line); (2) the ordinary-course standard tightness; (3) the materiality thresholds; (4) the consent timeframe (silence = deemed consent after [10] business days is buyer-aggressive); (5) post-signing flexibility for normal-course strategic moves the seller did not anticipate.

Clause 10 — Reverse Termination Fee / Limited Guarantee

Clause: Reverse Termination Fee + sponsor Limited Guarantee
"10.1 Reverse Termination Fee. If this Agreement is terminated (i) by the Seller pursuant to Clause [9.X(a)] (Buyer regulatory failure), or (ii) by either Party pursuant to Clause [9.X(b)] (Long-Stop Date) where, at the time of such termination, the only outstanding Conditions Precedent are antitrust, foreign-investment, or other regulatory clearances, then the Buyer shall pay to the Seller, within [10] Business Days of such termination, an amount equal to 4.0% of the Purchase Price (the 'Reverse Termination Fee') as liquidated damages and not as a penalty, which shall constitute the Seller's sole and exclusive remedy against the Buyer and any Buyer Affiliate, sponsor, financing source, equity investor, or other Buyer-Side Person in respect of such termination, and the Seller hereby irrevocably waives any other claim or remedy …

10.2 Limited Guarantee. The payment obligations of the Buyer under Clause 10.1 are guaranteed by [Sponsor], pursuant to the Limited Guarantee delivered concurrently with this Agreement, up to a maximum aggregate amount of [an amount equal to the Reverse Termination Fee plus reimbursable expenses up to € [X]] (the 'Limited Guarantee Cap'), beyond which neither [Sponsor] nor any of its Affiliates shall have any liability whatsoever in connection with this Agreement or the transactions contemplated hereby."

Why this clause sits at the centre of PE deal architecture. The reverse termination fee (RTF) is the seller's protection against a buyer that can't (or won't) close — typically because regulatory clearance failed. It is paired with a limited guarantee: a sponsor-level guarantee that backstops the RTF without exposing the sponsor to broader deal liability. Together, they substitute monetary certainty for specific performance.

Phrase-by-phrase decode.

  • "4.0% of the Purchase Price" — RTF as a percentage of price. Market range: 3–8% for regulatory-failure RTFs in mid-market PE; sometimes 10%+ where antitrust risk is high. Symmetrically, a buyer-side break fee (paid by seller if it walks for an alternative buyer) is typically 1–4%. The gap (RTF higher than break fee) reflects the asymmetric leverage — sellers fear a buyer walking more than buyers fear a seller switching.
  • "as liquidated damages and not as a penalty" — the liquidated damages characterisation. Critical for enforceability: in common-law systems (UK, US), penalty clauses are unenforceable (the doctrine in Cavendish v Makdessi [2015] UKSC 67 in the UK, the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 356 in the US). To survive, the amount must be a genuine pre-estimate of loss — so this label is doing protective work.
  • "sole and exclusive remedy against the Buyer and any Buyer Affiliate, sponsor, financing source, equity investor, or other Buyer-Side Person" — the exclusive-remedy clause. Once the RTF is paid, the seller cannot sue for further damages, specific performance, or anything else. The list of protected persons is critical. Without "sponsor", the seller could go after the PE fund directly; without "financing source", the seller could sue the lenders for tortious interference. Each name on the list is a bullet dodged.
  • "the Seller hereby irrevocably waives any other claim or remedy" — the express waiver. Belt and braces: even where the exclusive-remedy clause might be read narrowly, the explicit waiver closes off other routes (tortious interference, equitable claims).
  • "guaranteed by [Sponsor], pursuant to the Limited Guarantee delivered concurrently" — the limited-guarantee mechanic. Why "limited"? Because the sponsor (a PE fund) does not want to sign a full guarantee that exposes it to all of the buyer's obligations. The guarantee is "limited" in two ways: (i) capped (next phrase); (ii) scope-limited to the RTF only.
  • "up to a maximum aggregate amount of [an amount equal to the Reverse Termination Fee plus reimbursable expenses up to € [X]] (the 'Limited Guarantee Cap')" — the Limited Guarantee Cap. The sponsor's exposure is capped at the RTF plus a pre-agreed expense reimbursement. The senior partner line "the limited guarantee cap is too low" lives here — meaning the cap sits below the seller's real exposure if the deal collapses for regulatory reasons.
  • "beyond which neither [Sponsor] nor any of its Affiliates shall have any liability whatsoever" — the no-recourse-beyond-cap clause. Protects the sponsor from being sued for the rest of its fund. PE deal-protection 101.

What gets argued. (1) RTF size; (2) the trigger events (regulatory only? Long-Stop Date generally? Buyer breach?); (3) the protected-persons list; (4) the Limited Guarantee Cap (RTF + expenses, or RTF only); (5) interaction with specific performance — is SP available before RTF is owed? in parallel? mutually exclusive?

Clause 11 — Entire-Agreement, Non-Reliance, Exclusive Remedy with Fraud Carve-out

Clause: Entire Agreement / Non-Reliance / Exclusive Remedy / Save-for-Fraud
"14.1 Entire Agreement. This Agreement, together with the Disclosure Letter and the other Transaction Documents, constitutes the entire agreement between the Parties with respect to the subject matter hereof and supersedes all prior negotiations, agreements, representations and understandings, whether written or oral, between the Parties relating to such subject matter.

14.2 Non-Reliance. Each of the Parties acknowledges and agrees that, in entering into this Agreement and the other Transaction Documents, it has not relied on any representation, warranty, statement, undertaking, assurance, promise or other commitment made by or on behalf of any other Party (or any person acting on behalf of any other Party) other than as expressly set out in this Agreement or any other Transaction Document, and waives all rights and remedies in connection with any such other representation, warranty, statement, undertaking, assurance, promise or commitment.

14.3 Exclusive Remedy. Save as expressly provided in this Agreement, the indemnification regime set out in Clause 7 shall be the sole and exclusive remedy of the Buyer in respect of any breach of this Agreement, and each of the Parties hereby waives, to the fullest extent permitted by Law, any and all other rights and remedies (whether in contract, tort, statute or otherwise), including any rescission, repudiation or termination right.

14.4 Fraud Carve-out. Notwithstanding any other provision of this Agreement, nothing in this Agreement shall limit or exclude any liability for, or remedy in respect of, fraud (including fraudulent misrepresentation)."

Why this clause architecture is "the closed system". Together, Clauses 14.1–14.4 produce what the source deck calls a "closed system": the SPA is the sole locus of remedy, statutory and tort claims are excluded, pre-contractual representations don't survive, and only fraud breaks out. The drafting is interlocking — pull one piece and the others may fail.

Phrase-by-phrase decode.

  • "supersedes all prior negotiations, agreements, representations and understandings" — the merger clause proper. Pre-contractual statements (e.g. management presentations, the Confidential Information Memorandum, side emails) do not survive into the SPA. But on its own, this clause does NOT exclude liability for misrepresentation — see Springwell Navigation v JP Morgan Chase [2010] EWCA Civ 1221 (UK). The merger clause needs the non-reliance clause to do the misrep job.
  • "each of the Parties acknowledges and agrees that, in entering into this Agreement … it has not relied on any representation … other than as expressly set out" — the non-reliance clause. This is the misrep killer. Under the UK Misrepresentation Act 1967, liability for misrepresentation requires the misrepresentee to have relied on the misrepresentation. By contractually disclaiming reliance, the buyer rules out the cause of action ex ante. UK case law (Springwell; AXA Sun Life [2012]) accepts non-reliance clauses subject to the reasonableness test under section 3 of the Misrepresentation Act / UCTA, but the modern trend is enforcement (as long as the clause is fairly negotiated between commercial parties).
  • "and waives all rights and remedies in connection with any such other representation" — belt-and-braces waiver. Even if the non-reliance disclaimer fails, the express waiver provides a second line.
  • "the indemnification regime set out in Clause 7 shall be the sole and exclusive remedy" — the exclusive-remedy clause for breach of the SPA itself. Channels all post-closing recourse through the indemnification regime — meaning all the cap, basket, de minimis and survival limits apply. Without this, a buyer could sue for damages outside the indemnification regime under common-law contract principles, evading the limits.
  • "each of the Parties hereby waives, to the fullest extent permitted by Law, any and all other rights and remedies (whether in contract, tort, statute or otherwise), including any rescission, repudiation or termination right" — the residual-remedies waiver. Lists each escape route the buyer could otherwise use. Rescission (the right to unwind the contract — extreme in M&A; means returning the shares for the price); repudiation / termination (the right to treat the contract as discharged for breach). All gone. The buyer is left with damages under Clause 7 only.
  • "Notwithstanding any other provision of this Agreement, nothing in this Agreement shall limit or exclude any liability for, or remedy in respect of, fraud (including fraudulent misrepresentation)" — the fraud carve-out: the "save-for-fraud" line. This phrase is constitutionally required in UK practice. A clause excluding liability for fraud is unenforceable as a matter of public policy in England (HIH Casualty v Chase Manhattan Bank [2003] UKHL 6); a clause that purports to exclude fraud is read down in any event. Modern drafters include the carve-out expressly to (i) ensure the non-reliance and exclusive-remedy clauses survive challenge, (ii) signal good faith. The negotiation is the carve-out's scope: "fraud" only? "fraud or fraudulent misrepresentation"? "fraud, gross negligence or wilful misconduct"? The wider, the more it eats the closed system.

What gets argued. (1) Whether the closed system applies to all remedies or only some (specific performance, injunctive relief sometimes carved back to the buyer); (2) the precise definition of "fraud" (intent? recklessness? wilful blindness?); (3) whether the carve-out covers fraud by the seller's advisors or only by the seller itself; (4) interaction with W&I insurance — the W&I policy may have its own fraud exclusion that is not symmetrical with the SPA's carve-out.

Drafting decoder cheat-sheet — words that look interchangeable but allocate risk

The single biggest comprehension gap for a non-lawyer reading an SPA is failing to register that synonyms-in-English are not synonyms-on-the-page. The table below pairs the words you will read against the risk-allocation effect.

Drafting word / phraseWhat it does
"Best endeavours" (UK) / "reasonable best efforts" (US Delaware)Highest rung of the efforts ladder. Promisor must do everything a prudent and determined man, acting in his own interests, would do (IBM UK v Rockware Glass [1980]). May require the promisor to incur material commercial harm. In US Delaware, "reasonable best efforts" is the equivalent — though the US courts have been more reluctant than English courts to imply specific obligations.
"All reasonable endeavours" (UK)Middle rung. The promisor must explore all reasonable courses of action but is not required to subordinate its own commercial interests to the same extent as "best endeavours". Rhodia International Holdings v Huntsman International [2007] EWHC 292 (Comm) treats "all reasonable endeavours" as approximately equivalent to "best endeavours" in some contexts — drafting precision matters.
"Reasonable endeavours" (UK) / "commercially reasonable efforts" (US Delaware)Lowest rung. Promisor takes steps a reasonable similarly-situated commercial person would take, weighing its own interest. Does not require commercial sacrifice. In US Delaware, "commercially reasonable efforts" is the equivalent and is the default for most contractual undertakings short of "hell-or-high-water".
"Hell-or-high-water" (covenant on regulatory clearance)Strongest possible buyer commitment. Buyer must take any and all actions — divestitures, behavioural undertakings, abandoning markets — to obtain antitrust / FDI clearance. No carve-outs. Used where seller insists on regulatory certainty. Module 08.
"Fairly disclosed" (against a Disclosure Letter)Seller-friendly disclosure standard. A matter is "fairly disclosed" if it is identifiable from the Disclosure Letter on a reasonable reading, even if it is not specifically tied to the warranty it disclaims. The whole Disclosure Letter is the universe. New Hearts v Cosmopolitan Investments [1997] 2 BCLC 249 (Scotland) treats fair disclosure as requiring sufficient detail to identify the nature and scope of the matter.
"Specifically disclosed" / "disclosed against this Warranty"Buyer-friendly disclosure standard. The Disclosure Letter must identify each matter against the specific warranty it disclaims. One-to-one matching. The seller cannot bury a disclosure deep in the letter and expect it to qualify a different warranty. Compare to "fairly disclosed" — radically narrower universe.
"In all material respects" (bring-down or rep qualifier)Seller-friendly qualifier. A rep is "true in all material respects" if any inaccuracy is not material. A bring-down "in all material respects" allows the rep to be slightly off without failing the closing condition. Note: Delaware courts read "material respects" as a higher bar than "MAE" (Material Adverse Effect) — Akorn v Fresenius distinguished. Buyer counter: a materiality scrape strips this qualifier for damages purposes.
Unqualified (no "material" or "MAE")Buyer-friendly. The rep is true (or breached) in absolute terms. Any inaccuracy is breach. Used most often for fundamental reps (title, capacity) and in the post-bring-down indemnification regime.
"To the Seller's Knowledge" (actual)Seller-friendly. Limits the rep to what named individuals actually consciously know. Used where the seller cannot reasonably be expected to verify (e.g., third-party compliance, contingent litigation).
"To the Seller's Knowledge after due and careful inquiry" (constructive)Buyer-friendlier. Imposes a duty of investigation on the named individuals. They must ask their direct reports and review the books. Knowledge gaps caused by failure to inquire are imputed.
"To the best knowledge of the Seller"Ambiguous — avoid. Could be read as actual knowledge (UK case law leans here) or constructive (some civil-law and US courts). Modern drafting practice is to use a defined "Seller's Knowledge" term explicitly.
"Tipping basket" (basket recover-from-dollar-one)Buyer-friendly. Once the basket threshold is crossed, the seller pays from euro one. The basket is gating but not consuming.
"Deductible" / "true deductible"Seller-friendly. Once the threshold is crossed, only the excess is recoverable; the threshold itself is gone forever. The buyer absorbs the first threshold-amount of loss.
"Sweep the basket"Buyer-friendly. Sub-de-minimis claims (claims below the per-claim floor) still count toward the basket threshold, even if they cannot themselves be recovered. Without sweep, sub-de-minimis claims fall away entirely.
"Indemnify, defend, and hold harmless"US three-verb formula. Indemnify: pay the loss. Defend: take over and fund the legal defence of third-party claims. Hold harmless: in Delaware/NY majority, synonym for indemnify; minority view treats it as a separate counter-claim shield. UK practice often says "indemnify and keep indemnified" instead.
"Save for fraud" / "save as expressly provided""Save for fraud" — the universal carve-out from any limitation clause for fraud (constitutionally required in UK; HIH Casualty). "Save as expressly provided" — the more narrow drafting savings, preserving only what the rest of the agreement allows.
"Including without limitation" vs "including"In English law, "including" alone has been held by some authorities (e.g. Hindcastle v Barbara Attenborough [1997]) to be exhaustive in some contexts; "including without limitation" makes the non-exhaustive intention explicit. Modern UK drafting still uses "including" for brevity but the cautious drafter writes "including, without limitation," each time.
"Shall" vs "will" vs "agrees to" vs "undertakes to"In strict legal drafting, "shall" creates an obligation; "will" describes a future state; "agrees to" / "undertakes to" both create obligations. Modern plain-English drafting often replaces "shall" with "must" or "agrees to". Read for consistency in the document — drift between the verbs can suggest sloppy drafting and create ambiguity.
"Material" (undefined)Undefined "material" in UK practice means "of substantive importance to a reasonable person" — a contextual standard. In US Delaware, "material" in the M&A context tracks the TSC v Northway securities-law gloss (substantial likelihood of importance to a reasonable investor) but is fact-specific. The same word in the same SPA can mean different things depending on context — read each instance against the specific clause.

Reading-protocol summary — the four questions to ask on every clause

  1. Who promises what? Identify the promisor (seller? buyer? both?), the type of promise (covenant? rep? warranty? indemnity?), and the substance of the promise.
  2. When does the promise bite? Identify the temporal trigger — at signing, at closing, during the interim period, post-closing for a defined survival period.
  3. What qualifiers apply? Identify materiality, knowledge, fair-disclosure, and any specific carve-outs. Each qualifier is a slice of risk transferred from one party to the other.
  4. What is the remedy if it's broken? Damages? Indemnity? Termination? Specific performance? Inside or outside the cap and basket? Exclusive or in addition to common-law remedies?

Run these four questions across every clause in any drafted SPA. The drafted clauses above all answer the four questions differently — that's the point. Drafting is the calibration of the answers.

Module recap

  • Read SPA clauses in five passes — definitions, qualifiers, carve-outs, cross-references, remedies. Senior associates do not read once; they read five times, looking for different things each time.
  • The Loss definition is the keystone of the indemnification regime. Watch for "direct consequence" causation, the consequential-damages exclusion (which kills lost-profit and multiplier claims), and which persons are on the indemnified side.
  • The MAC / MAE definition is the most-negotiated paragraph in any SPA. The negotiation is in the carve-outs (pandemics, war, regulatory change) and the disproportionate-impact carve-back. The word "prospects" alone shifts who wears foreseeable-but-unrealised risk.
  • The Permitted Leakage / no-leakage covenant is the locked-box's beating heart — without it the seller could strip cash out between Reference Date and closing. The leakage indemnity sits outside the cap and basket, with its own short claims period.
  • The single most-leveraged definition in the SPA is the knowledge-group definition. Move from actual to constructive knowledge and you have doubled the seller's effective liability surface. The buyer's parallel move is a knowledge scrape that strips the qualifier for indemnification purposes.
  • Tipping basket vs true deductible is a one-sentence change with deal-size economic consequences. Sub-de-minimis sweep into the basket is the second sentence-change. Fundamental-rep cap (uncapped vs 100% of price) is the third.
  • Sandbagging defaults differ by governing law (Delaware pro, English anti). Modern SPAs choose explicitly. The express clause survives even after closing-condition waivers — so the buyer can waive a CP and retain damages.
  • The closed system — entire-agreement plus non-reliance plus exclusive remedy plus save-for-fraud — channels all post-closing recourse into the indemnification regime. The fraud carve-out is constitutionally required (HIH Casualty in UK; public-policy floor everywhere).
  • "Best endeavours" / "reasonable best efforts" / "all reasonable endeavours" / "commercially reasonable efforts" / "hell-or-high-water" form a graduated efforts ladder. Picking the rung is a deliberate allocation of regulatory and commercial risk.
